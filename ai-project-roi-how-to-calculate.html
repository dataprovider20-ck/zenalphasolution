How to Work Out the ROI of an AI Project Before You Build It

Published 4 November 2026 · 7 min read · By Zen Alpha Solution

Book Your Free Consultation

Most AI proposals arrive with a promise and no arithmetic. "Save up to 70% of your team's time" sounds wonderful until someone asks which team, which task and compared with what. If you are about to spend real money on an AI project, an hour with a spreadsheet beforehand will tell you more than any demo.

This is the method we would use on a napkin. It will not give you a precise forecast, and nobody can. It will tell you whether the project is worth a pilot, which is the decision you actually need to make.

Start with one process, not with the technology

"Use AI in finance" is not a project. "Extract line items from supplier invoices and enter them in the accounting system" is. The narrower the process, the easier it is to measure, and the less likely you are to pay for a tool that does a bit of everything and nothing well.

Pick a process that is repetitive, happens often, follows fairly clear rules and currently eats noticeable hours. Invoice entry, first-line support questions, lead qualification, document checking and report preparation all fit. Tasks that need fresh judgement every time, or that happen twice a year, rarely do.

Get four baseline numbers

You cannot measure improvement without a starting point. For the process you chose, write down:

  • Volume: how many times it happens per month.
  • Time per item: how long one takes, from start to finish, including checking.
  • Error rate: how often the output has to be fixed, and how long fixing takes.
  • Cost of an hour: what an hour of that person's time costs you, including salary, benefits and overhead, not just the pay slip figure.

Do not guess these. Ask the people who do the work, then time ten real examples. Estimates from managers are usually optimistic about how fast a task is and pessimistic about how often it goes wrong.

Count every cost, including the boring ones

The build quote is the smallest part of the picture for some projects and the largest for others, so list everything.

  • Build: design, development, integration with your existing systems and testing.
  • Running costs: model usage fees, hosting and any tools the system depends on. Many are billed by volume, so ask for a figure at your volume, not an average.
  • Human review: almost every useful AI system still needs a person to check some of its output. That time belongs in the cost column.
  • Maintenance: your forms, suppliers and policies change. Someone has to keep the system aligned, and that someone has a price.
  • Change management: training, a period of running old and new side by side, and the dip in productivity while people adjust.

Count benefits honestly

This is where most spreadsheets go wrong. "We save 120 hours a month" is only a benefit if those hours turn into something: a hire you no longer need, overtime you stop paying, or time your team spends on work that earns money. If 120 hours are saved but nothing changes, you have a nicer working life and no financial return. That can still be worth it, but call it what it is.

Other benefits are real but harder to price: fewer errors, faster response times, happier customers, deals not lost because someone replied on time. Include them separately and be conservative. A good test is whether you would stand behind the number in front of your finance head.

A worked example

The figures below are invented for illustration. Swap in your own.

A distributor in Pune processes 1,500 supplier invoices a month. A clerk spends about 7 minutes on each, reading it and keying the data into the accounting software. That is 10,500 minutes, or 175 hours a month. With an hour costing ₹300 all-in, the process costs ₹52,500 a month.

An AI extraction system reads each invoice and fills in the fields. Suppose 80% of invoices go through cleanly and need only a one-minute check, while the other 20% are messy and still take the full 7 minutes. The new workload is 1,200 invoices at 1 minute plus 300 invoices at 7 minutes, which is 3,300 minutes, or 55 hours. Hours saved: 120. Value at ₹300 an hour: ₹36,000 a month.

Now the costs. Say model usage and hosting come to ₹8,000 a month, and monitoring and upkeep another ₹6,000. That leaves a net benefit of ₹22,000 a month. If the build costs ₹4,00,000, payback takes about 18 months.

Eighteen months is not a disaster, but it is not an obvious yes either. Many finance teams would ask for a faster return.

Now change one input. The same business grows, or a sister company joins in, and volume reaches 5,000 invoices a month. The manual process takes about 583 hours. After automation it needs 4,000 invoices at 1 minute and 1,000 at 7 minutes, which is 11,000 minutes, or about 183 hours. Savings: 400 hours, worth ₹1,20,000. Running costs rise to perhaps ₹20,000. Net benefit: ₹1,00,000 a month, and the ₹4,00,000 build pays back in four months.

The lesson is that volume decides almost everything. The same technology is a questionable investment at one volume and a clear one at another. This is exactly the kind of thing a demo will never show you.

Stress-test the answer

Run the numbers three ways: a cautious case, an expected case and a good case. In the cautious case, assume that only 60% of invoices go through cleanly and that running costs are 50% higher than quoted. If the project still makes sense, good. If it only works in the best case, you have found the risk before paying for it.

Also ask what happens when the system is wrong. In the invoice example, a wrong amount could mean a wrong payment. If a mistake is costly, the review step has to be stronger, and that eats into the savings. Build that back into the model.

Run a pilot with a stop rule

A pilot is how you replace estimates with real figures. Choose a slice of the work, such as one supplier group or one product line. Define in advance what success looks like, for example "at least 70% of invoices processed with only a quick check, and fewer errors than the manual process". Define the failure line as well. If the pilot misses it after a fixed period, you stop.

Writing the stop rule down before you start matters. Without it, projects that are not working tend to continue, because so much has already been spent.

When the answer is no

Sometimes the best result of this exercise is a clear no. The volume is too small, the process changes every month, the data is a mess, or a simple fix would do the job. A cleaner form, a template or a small script can beat an AI system on cost for a surprising number of tasks. Any honest vendor should be willing to tell you when that is the case.

Frequently asked questions

How long does an AI project usually take to pay back?

It depends almost entirely on volume and on how much of the process can be handled without human review. Work it out with your own numbers rather than relying on a general figure.

Should I include "soft" benefits in the ROI?

Track them, but keep them out of the headline figure. Decide the go or no-go on benefits you can measure, and treat the rest as a bonus.

Can small businesses get a return from AI?

Yes, usually with narrow, inexpensive projects such as support replies or document handling, rather than large custom builds. At small volumes, ready-made tools often beat custom development.
