Most founders who consider hiring a development team in India ask the same question first: is it cheaper? It is the wrong place to start. The better questions are whether your project suits a remote team, and how to set the work up so the distance stops mattering.

This guide is written for founders and small-business owners in the UK and the US. It covers the engagement models, how to check a team before you commit, how the time zones actually line up, what belongs in the contract and what a sensible first 90 days looks like. It does not promise savings, because the real outcome depends far more on how clearly you brief the work than on where the developers sit.

## Is this the right move for your project?

Remote delivery from India tends to work well when:

- The scope can be written down: screens, behaviour, rules and what "finished" means.
- You can review work asynchronously, from demos, written updates and a staging site.
- You are building a web or mobile product, an internal tool, an integration or an automation, rather than something that needs people physically on site.
- You, or someone you trust, can make product decisions quickly when the team asks a question.

It tends to work badly when:

- The idea is still changing every week, and nobody owns decisions.
- You need real-time collaboration all day, every day, in a US or UK time zone.
- You want to hand over a vague idea and receive a finished product without being involved.
- Your project involves regulated data and you have not yet worked out your obligations.

If the second list sounds like you, the fix is usually a clearer plan, not a different country.

## Three ways to work with an India-based team

The commercial model matters as much as the team. These are the three common ones.

**1. Fixed-scope project.** You agree what is built, by when, in stages. This suits a well-defined MVP, a website or a single integration. The risk is that change is awkward: every new idea becomes a change request, so the scope document has to be good.

**2. Dedicated team.** You pay monthly for a team (or a few people) who work on your product continuously. This suits a product that will keep evolving. The risk is drift: without a clear roadmap, a dedicated team will happily keep busy. You need someone steering.

**3. Staff augmentation.** Individual developers join your own team and follow your processes, tools and standups. This suits companies that already have a technical lead and need more hands. The risk is management time: you manage them like employees.

If you are unsure, start with a small fixed-scope piece, such as a prototype or one feature. It tells you more about a team than any sales call.

## What you should always keep in-house

Whatever model you choose, hold on to four things:

1. **Product decisions.** The team can advise, but you decide what gets built and in what order.
2. **Ownership of accounts.** The code repository, cloud hosting, domain, app-store accounts, analytics and third-party services should be registered in your company's name, with the team added as users. Never the other way round.
3. **Acceptance.** You, or someone independent, decides whether work is done.
4. **The roadmap.** Keep it in a place you control.

A founder who keeps these four can change vendors without losing the product. One who does not is stuck.

## How to check a development company before you commit

Reading a portfolio tells you little. Ask these instead, and note whether the answers are specific.

1. Can you describe a project like mine, what went wrong, and how you handled it?
2. Who exactly will work on my project, and how long have they worked together?
3. How will I see progress, and how often?
4. How do you write and agree requirements before coding starts?
5. What does your testing look like, and who tests before I see a release?
6. What happens to my code, accounts and documentation if we stop working together?
7. Who answers messages when a developer is on leave or unwell?
8. How do you handle a change in scope halfway through?

Good teams ask questions back. If a company quotes a price within minutes of your first message, without asking anything about your users or your goals, treat the quote as a guess.

Ask for a reference you can speak to directly, and a short paid trial task if the project is large.

## Time zones: what the numbers actually look like

India is on UTC+5:30 all year and does not change its clocks. The UK and the US do, and they change on different dates, so the gap shifts a few times a year.

| Your location | Winter gap (India ahead by) | Summer gap (India ahead by) |
|---|---|---|
| UK | 5 hours 30 minutes | 4 hours 30 minutes |
| US East (New York) | 10 hours 30 minutes | 9 hours 30 minutes |
| US West (California) | 13 hours 30 minutes | 12 hours 30 minutes |

For a few weeks in March and in October or November, the UK and US are on different clock settings, so the gap changes by an hour between them. Check any recurring meeting during those weeks.

What this means in practice:

- **UK:** a good overlap. A UK morning is an Indian afternoon, so a team working roughly 2:30 pm to 6:30 pm in India meets a UK business day from 9:00 am to 1:00 pm in winter.
- **US East:** a smaller, evening overlap for the Indian team. A 9:00 am New York start is early evening in India, so overlap usually means the team working a later shift on some days.
- **US West:** a very small overlap. Plan to work asynchronously with one or two short calls a week.

The answer is not to pretend the gap does not exist. It is to design around it. Agree a fixed overlap window at the start, keep calls inside it, and rely on written updates for everything else.

## A communication rhythm that works across time zones

Teams that work well across distance usually keep the rhythm simple:

- **One shared channel** for day-to-day questions, and one place for decisions.
- **A short written update every working day:** what was done, what is next, what is blocked.
- **A weekly call** inside the overlap window, to review progress and agree priorities.
- **A demo every one to two weeks,** on a staging site you can click through yourself.
- **A decision log,** so nothing agreed on a call is lost.

The most useful habit is asking for blockers early and in writing. A question asked at the end of the Indian working day can be answered in your morning, so the team starts the next day unblocked.

## Write requirements so distance does not matter

Distance punishes vague instructions. A few tools make the difference:

- **User stories** with acceptance criteria. "As a customer I can reset my password by email" is testable. "Make login nice" is not.
- **Design files and examples.** A screenshot of something you like beats a paragraph describing it.
- **A definition of done,** agreed once: tested, reviewed, deployed to staging and documented.
- **A short glossary** for any business terms the team may not know.

Spending a week on this before coding starts often saves more time than any process improvement later.

## Ownership, confidentiality and the contract

A good contract is boring and clear. Make sure it covers:

- **Intellectual property.** All code and designs created for you are assigned to your company on payment.
- **Confidentiality (NDA)** for the company and for the individuals who work on your project.
- **Scope, milestones and acceptance criteria,** with payment linked to milestones, not one large payment upfront.
- **Support after launch:** bug-fix period, response times and how ongoing work is priced.
- **Exit terms:** how the code, accounts and documentation are handed over, and how quickly.
- **Subcontracting:** whether the company may pass your work to others, and what then applies.

This is general information, not legal advice. Ask a lawyer who knows your jurisdiction to review the final agreement.

## Data protection and security basics

You do not need to be a security expert, but you do need to set the ground rules:

- Give each developer their **own login** with the **minimum access** they need, and remove access when they leave the project.
- Keep **real customer data out of development and test environments.** Use dummy data.
- Use **two-factor authentication** on code, cloud and admin accounts.
- Keep **secrets** (API keys, passwords) out of the code repository.
- Take **regular backups** and check that you can restore them.

If your product handles personal data of UK or EU residents, UK GDPR and GDPR rules on sending data outside the UK or EU apply, and you will usually need an approved transfer mechanism in the contract. For US health, finance or children's data, other rules may apply. Confirm the details with your own legal or compliance adviser before real data reaches the team.

## What drives the cost (without the sales talk)

Rather than comparing hourly rates, compare what you get. The main cost drivers are:

- **How clear the scope is.** Unclear scope means rework, and rework is where budgets disappear.
- **The seniority of the people,** and how much of their time is on your project.
- **Project length.** Longer engagements usually make the team more efficient as they learn your product.
- **Changes during the build.** They are normal, but they cost time.
- **How much overlap and management** the engagement needs.

A low quote is not automatically a good deal, and a high one is not automatically better. Ask each company to explain what is included, what is not, and what could change the price.

## Red flags

- A fixed price and date with no questions asked about your project.
- Reluctance to let you own the code or the accounts.
- No clear person responsible for your project.
- Progress reports that say "going well" but cannot be shown on a working staging site.
- Pressure to pay most of the fee upfront.
- A promise of guaranteed results or guaranteed rankings, sales or users.

## The first 90 days

A simple plan keeps expectations clear:

**Days 1-10: set up.** Create accounts in your company's name, agree the communication rhythm and overlap window, share the brief, and sign the NDA and agreement.

**Days 10-30: a small first milestone.** Build one meaningful, testable piece. Review the quality of the code, the communication and how the team handles feedback.

**Days 30-60: build steadily.** Keep the weekly call and fortnightly demos. Track what was promised against what was delivered.

**Days 60-90: review and decide.** Look at quality, speed and how well the team understands your business. Decide whether to continue, adjust the team, or change the model.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is outsourcing software development to India safe?**
It can be, if you set the ground rules: your own accounts, a signed NDA and IP assignment, least-privilege access, no real customer data in test environments, and a clear exit plan. The risk comes from vague arrangements, not from the country.

**Do I need to be technical to work with an India-based team?**
No, but you need to be clear about what you want and quick to decide. If you are not technical, get independent technical oversight to review the plan and the work.

**What is the best engagement model for a first project?**
A small fixed-scope piece, such as a prototype or one feature. It shows you how the team communicates and delivers before you commit to something larger.

**How do I handle the time difference?**
Agree a fixed overlap window, hold a weekly call inside it, and rely on written updates between calls. The overlap is better for the UK than for the US East Coast and smallest for the US West Coast.
