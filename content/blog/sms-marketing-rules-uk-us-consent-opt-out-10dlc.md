Text messages get read. That is exactly why they are regulated. A badly planned SMS campaign can lead to complaints, blocked messages and, in the worst cases, serious fines or lawsuits.

This guide is for small business owners, marketers and developers who send (or plan to send) text messages to customers in the **United Kingdom** and the **United States**. It explains the main rules in plain English and gives you a checklist to run before you press send.

## Quick answer

- **UK:** you need specific, opt-in consent to send marketing texts to individuals (PECR and UK GDPR), unless the narrow **soft opt-in** applies to existing customers. Always identify yourself and include an opt-out.
- **US:** marketing texts need **prior express written consent** (TCPA), a working STOP opt-out, sensible sending hours, and **A2P 10DLC registration** for business texting from standard numbers.
- **Both:** keep proof of consent, honour opt-outs immediately and never use a bought list.

One honest note first: we are a technology team, not a law firm. This is general information, last checked in November 2026. Rules change, and your situation may be different, so have a qualified lawyer review your campaign before you launch.

## There are two sets of rules, not one

Most confusion comes from mixing up two different things:

1. **The law.** It decides who you are allowed to text, and what you must do before and after. In the UK this is mainly the Privacy and Electronic Communications Regulations (PECR) together with UK GDPR. In the US it is mainly the Telephone Consumer Protection Act (TCPA), plus some state laws.
2. **The delivery rules.** Mobile networks and carriers decide whether your message actually reaches the phone. In the US, this includes the carrier registration system known as 10DLC.

You need to satisfy both. Following the law does not guarantee delivery, and being registered with carriers does not make an unlawful message lawful.

## Key terms in 30 seconds

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|

## First question: is it a marketing message?

The rules are strictest for marketing, so it helps to separate two kinds of message.

| Type | Examples | What it needs |
|---|---|---|
| Service or transactional | Order confirmation, delivery update, appointment reminder, one-time passcode | Lighter rules, but still accurate and relevant |
| Marketing or promotional | Discounts, sale announcements, new product news, "we miss you" offers | Consent (or an exemption), a clear sender, and an easy opt-out |

Be careful with mixed messages. If a delivery update also pushes a discount code, regulators can treat the whole message as marketing. Keep service messages about the service.

## The UK: PECR and UK GDPR

In the UK, a marketing text counts as "electronic mail" under PECR. The ICO, the UK data regulator, publishes detailed guidance on the rules. The short version:

**You need consent to text individuals.** That means a clear, positive action by the person (for example, ticking an unticked box) that is specific to text messages. A vague tick-box such as "send me marketing" is not specific enough. Consent given for phone calls does not automatically cover text messages.

**The soft opt-in is the main exception.** You may text existing customers without fresh consent only if all of these are true:

- You collected their contact details during a sale (or negotiations for a sale).
- You are promoting your own **similar** products or services.
- You gave them a simple chance to refuse when you collected their details, and you give them that chance again in **every** message.

The soft opt-in is narrow. A visitor who only filled in an enquiry form and never bought anything is not covered. A purchased list is definitely not covered.

**Businesses are treated differently from individuals.** Limited companies, LLPs and public bodies are "corporate subscribers", and you can send them unsolicited marketing without consent, although you must still identify yourself and offer an opt-out. Sole traders and some partnerships are treated as individuals, so consent rules apply to them.

**Every marketing text must identify your business and carry a working opt-out**, even when you are texting a company.

**The penalties are no longer small.** Since 5 February 2026, the maximum fine for breaching PECR matches UK GDPR levels: the higher of £17.5 million or 4% of global annual turnover, up from £500,000 before. The ICO also keeps using its powers: in January 2026 it fined two companies a combined £225,000 for sending marketing messages without valid consent or without meeting the soft opt-in conditions.

## The US: TCPA, written consent and quiet hours

In the US, the main federal law is the TCPA, enforced by the Federal Communications Commission (FCC) and, importantly, through private lawsuits.

**Consent depends on the message type.** Automated or software-sent texts generally need the recipient's prior express consent. For **marketing** texts, the standard is higher: **prior express written consent**. In practice, that means a signed agreement (an electronic signature or checkbox is fine) that clearly authorises you to send marketing texts to that number, and that says agreeing is not a condition of buying anything.

**Quiet hours.** The FCC's telemarketing rules limit contact to between 8 am and 9 pm in the recipient's local time. There is an ongoing legal debate about whether this applies to texts sent with written consent, but the safe approach is simple: do not send marketing texts outside those hours, and check the time zone of the recipient, not yours.

**Opt-outs must be honoured.** The FCC has recognised replies such as STOP, QUIT, END, REVOKE, OPT OUT, CANCEL and UNSUBSCRIBE as valid ways to withdraw consent, and people can also revoke consent in other reasonable ways. The FCC's 2024 rule allows a reasonable time, not exceeding 10 business days, to process a request. The practical habit is much stricter: stop immediately and keep a suppression list. The FCC has also been revising how far one opt-out reaches across different types of message, so check the current position before you automate it.

**Lawsuits are the real risk.** The TCPA lets individuals sue. Statutory damages are $500 per violating message, and up to $1,500 per message if the violation is found to be willful. Multiply that across a list of thousands and the danger of a class action becomes obvious.

**States add their own rules.** Several US states have their own text marketing laws (Florida and Texas, for example, have expanded their requirements in recent years), so a national campaign may need more than the federal checklist.

## US delivery: what 10DLC is and why it matters

If you send business text messages from a standard 10-digit US phone number, carriers require you to register. This system is called **A2P 10DLC** (application-to-person, 10-digit long code).

- You register your **brand** (who you are) and each **campaign** (what you send and why) through The Campaign Registry.
- You describe your use case, give sample messages and explain how people opt in.
- Carriers use this information to decide whether to deliver, throttle or block your messages. SMS providers report that carriers now block unregistered 10DLC traffic.
- Approval usually takes a few business days, so register before your launch date, not the day of it.

Other routes exist, such as toll-free numbers (which have their own verification) and short codes, but they come with their own rules. Registration is not a legal shield: your messages must still meet the TCPA and the carriers' content rules.

## Bulk SMS delivery in the UK

UK delivery is less formal, but the same logic applies. Your SMS provider and the mobile networks watch for spam, so use a clear sender name, stay relevant and do not send to anyone who has opted out. Expect your provider to ask what you plan to send.

## SMS marketing compliance checklist for UK and US campaigns

1. **Know your message type.** Is it service or marketing? Do not hide promotions inside service messages.
2. **Collect consent properly.** Use an unticked opt-in box that names SMS, and explain what people will receive.
3. **Keep proof.** Save the time, the form or page used, the exact wording shown and the phone number for each opt-in.
4. **Identify your business** in every message.
5. **Offer an easy opt-out** in every marketing message ("Reply STOP to unsubscribe") and handle it immediately.
6. **Keep a suppression list** and check it before every send.
7. **Respect sending hours,** and use the recipient's local time.
8. **Never use a bought or rented list** unless you have a lawyer's written view that the consent is valid for your use. In most cases, third-party consent will not meet the UK or US standard.
9. **Send what you promised,** at the frequency you promised.
10. **Register your sender** where required (US 10DLC) before you launch.
11. **Have your lawyer review** your forms, wording and process.

## Sample wording you can adapt

These are illustrations, not legal text. Adapt them to your business and have them reviewed.

**UK sign-up checkbox (unticked by default):**

> ☐ Yes, I'd like to receive offers and updates from [Business Name] by text message. You can opt out at any time by replying STOP.

**US sign-up checkbox (unticked by default):**

> ☐ By checking this box, I agree to receive recurring marketing text messages from [Business Name] at the number provided. Consent is not a condition of purchase. Message and data rates may apply. Message frequency varies. Reply STOP to cancel or HELP for help.

**A marketing text:**

> [Business Name]: Our winter sale starts Friday. Browse the offers at example.com/sale. Reply STOP to opt out.

**A service text:**

> [Business Name]: Your order #1234 has shipped and should arrive on Thursday. Track it at example.com/track.

## Mistakes that cause trouble

| Mistake | Why it is a problem | Better approach |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-ticked consent boxes | Not a positive action in the UK, and weak evidence in the US | Leave boxes unticked |
| Burying consent in terms and conditions | Consent must be clear and specific | Use a separate, plain-language checkbox |
| Texting everyone who ever enquired | Enquiry is not a sale, so the UK soft opt-in does not apply | Ask for consent |
| No proof of opt-in | You cannot defend a complaint without records | Log every opt-in |
| Ignoring "STOP" variations | People revoke consent in different words | Handle STOP, UNSUBSCRIBE, CANCEL, END and QUIT, and read replies |
| Sending in the middle of the night | Quiet hours and customer irritation | Send within sensible local hours |
| Using one big shared list | Consent for one business is not consent for yours | Only text people who asked to hear from you |
| Launching before 10DLC approval | Messages get blocked | Register early |

## What about WhatsApp?

WhatsApp is popular for customer communication, especially in the UK, and it has its own rules. Its business messaging policy expects you to have the person's opt-in before sending business messages. The same principles apply: ask clearly, keep records, make it easy to stop. ## Frequently asked questions

### Can I text customers who gave me their number when they ordered?
For order-related updates, yes, service messages are generally fine. For promotions, you need consent in the US (written consent for marketing), and in the UK you need either consent or the soft opt-in conditions to be met.

### Can I buy a phone number list and text it?
This is very risky. In the UK, consent must be specific to you and to text messages, and in the US the written consent must be given to you. A list vendor's promise is rarely enough. Do not do it without legal advice.

### Do the rules apply to business-to-business texts?
In the UK, limited companies can be texted without prior consent, but you must still identify yourself and provide an opt-out, and sole traders are treated as individuals. In the US, the TCPA can apply to texts sent to a mobile number even when the topic is business.

### What is the difference between PECR and GDPR for SMS marketing?
PECR is the specific UK law about electronic marketing such as texts and emails, while UK GDPR governs how you handle personal data, including what counts as valid consent. For SMS marketing you must comply with both.

### What counts as a valid opt-in for text message marketing?
A clear, positive action such as ticking an unticked box that names SMS. In the US it must also be written consent that clearly authorises marketing texts to that number.

### Do I need 10DLC registration if I only send a few texts?
Business texting from a standard US 10-digit number needs registration whatever the volume. Your SMS provider should walk you through it.

### Does it matter where my SMS provider is based?
The rules generally follow the recipients and the sender of the marketing, not the location of the provider. Ask your provider which UK and US routes they support and whether they help with registration.

### How long should I keep consent records?
Keep them for as long as you are texting the person, and for a sensible period afterwards in case of a complaint. Ask your lawyer for the right period for your business.

### Is this legal advice?
No. It is general information to help you ask better questions. Always check your specific campaign with a qualified lawyer.
