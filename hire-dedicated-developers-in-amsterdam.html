Already International? Then Remote Fits Naturally

Many Amsterdam tech teams already work in English with colleagues from a dozen countries. Adding a developer based in India changes little about how the team communicates, and the time gap is small enough that the developer joins stand-ups, pairing sessions and planning live.

Scale-ups use dedicated developers to keep pace during growth phases, to cover specialist skills, or to protect the roadmap while permanent hiring takes its course.

Things to Agree in Advance

If your company has a works council, ask HR whether new tooling for tracking work needs its input. Agree IP assignment, confidentiality and data processing terms before access is granted, and keep accounts under your control.

The First Two Weeks: Onboarding a Dedicated Developer

A developer who joins your Amsterdam team does not start at full speed on day one, and no one should expect it. A planned first two weeks usually saves a month of slow progress.

Before day one, prepare access to the code repository, the project board, chat and a development environment. Delays in access are the commonest reason for a slow start. Write a short document that explains the product, who your users are and how a change goes from idea to release.

In week one, give the developer a small, real task, such as fixing a minor bug. It tests the whole path: running the code, making a change, getting a review and deploying. Pair them with a colleague for the first review so questions get answered quickly.

In week two, increase the size of the work and agree on how you will communicate: a daily stand-up, a weekly planning call and a clear place for questions. Hold a feedback talk at the end of the second week to adjust what is not working. Dedicated developers tend to settle in faster when someone on your team is named as their go-to contact.
