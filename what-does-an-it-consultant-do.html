What Does an IT Consultant Actually Do, and When Should You Hire One?

Published 4 November 2026 · 6 min read · By Zen Alpha Solution

Get a Free Consultation

"IT consultant" is one of those job titles that covers everything from someone who sets up office printers to a former CTO who advises on a nine-figure platform. If you have ever wondered what you would actually be paying for, you are asking the right question.

This article explains the work in plain terms: what consultants do, what you should walk away with, and how to tell when a consultant is worth hiring and when you are better off without one.

The short answer

An IT consultant gives you independent, expert advice about technology decisions, and sometimes helps you carry them out. The word "independent" is doing a lot of work there. A good consultant is not trying to sell you a particular product or fill a team with their own people. They are paid to look at your situation and tell you what they would do.

The kinds of work consultants do

Technology health checks

This is the "look under the bonnet" job. A consultant reviews your websites, applications, servers, cloud accounts, backups and access controls, and reports what is fragile, outdated or risky. It is a common first engagement because it produces a clear list of problems and an order in which to fix them.

Architecture and technology choices

Should you build or buy? Which database, which cloud provider, which platform? These choices are cheap to make and expensive to reverse. A consultant who has seen how similar decisions played out can save you from a costly detour.

Vendor and software selection

Choosing a CRM, ERP, e-commerce platform or development partner involves a pile of demos and promises. A consultant helps you write requirements, compare options on equal terms and ask the questions salespeople hope you will skip.

Cloud costs and migration

Cloud bills tend to creep up. A review can turn up unused servers, oversized instances and storage nobody remembers creating. For migrations, a consultant plans the sequence so that the business keeps running while the move happens.

Security basics

For many small and mid-sized companies, the real risks are unglamorous: shared passwords, no multi-factor authentication, untested backups, former staff who still have access. A consultant can put practical controls in place and prepare you for the security questionnaires larger customers send.

Project rescue

Sometimes a software project has gone off the rails: late, over budget, or delivered but not working as described. A consultant can review the code and the contract, say what state things are in, and recommend whether to fix, rebuild or walk away.

What you should actually receive

An engagement should end with something you can hold in your hands. Depending on the work, that is a written assessment, a prioritised list of recommendations with effort and cost estimates, an architecture diagram, a vendor comparison with scoring, a roadmap, or a set of policies and runbooks.

The test is simple: could someone else pick up the document and act on it? If the main deliverable is a slide deck full of generalities, ask for more. Good advice is specific to your systems, names the trade-offs and says what to do first.

Consultant, agency or in-house hire?

They solve different problems.

  • An in-house IT person or team is right when you have ongoing, daily needs that justify a salary. They know your business best but may have limited breadth.
  • An agency or development company builds things. Their incentive is to build, which is useful once you know what to build and slightly awkward when you are still deciding.
  • A consultant helps you decide and sets direction. They are most valuable before a big spend, or when you need an outside view of what you already have.

Many businesses use all three at different times. If you need ongoing senior leadership, not a one-off review, you may be looking for a virtual CTO instead, which is a closely related arrangement.

Warning signs when choosing a consultant

  • They recommend one product before understanding your problem. Ask how they are paid by vendors, if at all.
  • Vague deliverables. "Strategy workshops" without a defined output are a red flag.
  • No examples of similar work. You do not need identical experience, but you do need relevant experience, and a way to check it.
  • Heavy jargon, little explanation. A good consultant can explain a trade-off to a non-technical owner without making them feel small.
  • Promises of guaranteed outcomes. Technology work has uncertainty. Honest advisers say so.

How to prepare, so you pay for advice and not for searching

A little preparation shortens the engagement and sharpens the result. Before the first call, gather these five things:

  1. A list of the systems you use, who owns each one and roughly what each costs.
  2. Access to the people who know where things are, even if they are not technical.
  3. The two or three problems that actually hurt, in business terms: slow checkout, lost leads, month-end taking a week.
  4. Any existing contracts with developers, hosting providers and software vendors.
  5. A rough budget range and a deadline, even if both are soft.

How consultants charge

There are three common models. A fixed-price assessment suits a defined piece of work, such as a health check, because both sides know what is being delivered. Time and materials, billed hourly or daily, suits open-ended or exploratory work, but you should agree a cap or a review point. A monthly retainer suits ongoing advice for a set number of hours.

Whichever you choose, get the scope, the deliverables and the payment terms in writing. And ask what happens if you want to stop partway: a fair arrangement lets you leave without penalty after each stage.

When you do not need a consultant

If your need is small and specific, such as fixing a bug or building a defined feature, go straight to a developer. If your systems are simple and working, there may be nothing to advise on. And if you already know the answer and simply want it validated, one short call is usually enough, no full engagement required.

Frequently asked questions

How long does a typical IT consulting engagement last?

A focused health check or vendor selection often takes a few weeks. Ongoing advisory work runs month to month. The right length depends on the scope, so agree it up front.

Will a consultant implement the changes too?

Some do and some do not. Either is fine, but be clear in advance, because advice and delivery are separate jobs with separate fees.

Can a small business afford an IT consultant?

A short, fixed-scope engagement can cost far less than fixing a wrong decision later. Start with a single, well-defined question rather than a broad review.
